OPS-01 · Identity & Access

Identity Lifecycle Automation

HR-to-identity-provider pipeline · Okta Workflows, Workday, Slack, email

The problem

At a distributed technology company hiring across multiple countries, every new hire and every departure meant a slow, manual relay race between HR, IT, and whoever remembered which app needed a license revoked. Access landed late for new starters and lingered too long for people who had already left — visible friction coming in, and a quiet security risk going out.

How it works

1HR system marks a hire or termination
2Identity provider imports the change hourly
3Workflow sets manager, username & email
4Account staged, then activated near start date
5Apps provisioned or deprovisioned automatically
6Stakeholders notified in chat & email

The HR system doing the hiring is the single source of truth. An identity-provider job polls it hourly and imports anyone starting within the next week, staging — not activating — their account, so nothing goes live before it should. A short chain of workflows fills in what HR data leaves blank: deriving a username and work email from the person's preferred name with built-in collision handling, resolving a manager's email from an organizational ID, and queuing a scheduled job that flips the account to active and sends welcome messages within 24 hours of the real start date.

Downstream, every app with standards-based provisioning picks up the new account automatically; the handful that don't route to a documented manual checklist with the manager tagged in, so day-one access doesn't depend on someone's memory. Offboarding mirrors the same pipeline in reverse: a termination date deactivates the account at midnight — immediately for a short list of high-risk termination reasons — cascades through the same automated connections, transfers the departing person's files and calendar to their manager, and, after a 30-day recoverability window, deletes the account for good.

Decisions that mattered

Staging rather than activating accounts early stopped a recurring problem: new hires getting access — and Slack pings — days before their actual start date. On the way out, the 30-day delay before permanent deletion was a deliberate trade-off: long enough to recover from a mistaken termination or a legal hold, short enough that stale accounts don't quietly pile up. Anything without a standards-based deprovisioning hook got written down as a manual step with an owner, instead of being quietly assumed to be "handled."

Outcome

Standard hires now reach day one with working access and no manual ticket in the queue. Standard departures lose access the same day, automatically, instead of whenever someone got around to it. The exceptions — contractors, non-standard apps, unusual termination reasons — are now a short, explicit list instead of the whole process.

What it intentionally doesn't do

It doesn't replace judgment on contractor access tiers, and it doesn't pretend an app is deprovisioned if it isn't — unsupported apps are tracked on a checklist, never hidden.

OPS-02 · Identity & Access

Access Governance & SSO Architecture

Standards & documentation · SAML, SCIM, JIT provisioning

The problem

Every new SaaS app arrived with its own opinion about how single sign-on should work, and nobody had written down which pattern applied where. The result was inconsistent setups, a few apps that silently kept working after someone's access was supposedly removed, and a support desk that had to rediscover "who owns this tool" every time something broke.

How it works

1Classify the app by provisioning support
2Match it to one of three known patterns
3Build groups & assign members
4Document any gap in automatic deprovisioning
5Add it to the shared tool-ownership map

Rather than solving single sign-on one integration at a time, the work was to name and diagram the three patterns that actually exist in practice: apps with full account-lifecycle support, where sign-on, account creation, and deactivation all happen automatically; apps with sign-on only and just-in-time account creation, where the first login creates the account but nothing removes it later; and sign-on-only apps with no automatic account creation or removal at all.

Each pattern got its own swimlane runbook — identity-provider steps, authentication steps, and what happens inside the connected app — so a teammate configuring a new integration could match it to a pattern instead of guessing. Alongside that, every support and administration tool the team relied on (service requests, remote support, device management, asset tracking) got mapped into one diagram showing how a request actually flows from a person asking for help to a resolved ticket, across chat, email, and a self-service portal.

Decisions that mattered

The uncomfortable finding — that some apps keep a live, working login even after the identity provider marks someone as offboarded — didn't get swept under the rug. Those apps were named explicitly and added to a manual deprovisioning checklist, because a documented manual step beats an undocumented false sense of automation.

Outcome

New app integrations now start from one of three known patterns instead of a blank page, cutting the guesswork — and the back-and-forth — out of onboarding a new SaaS tool. The support-tool map became the reference the team actually opens when a request lands in the wrong queue.

What it intentionally doesn't do

It doesn't fix a vendor's limitations — some apps genuinely can't be auto-deprovisioned — but it makes those limitations visible and owned instead of invisible and assumed away.

OPS-03 · Automation & AI

AI-Assisted License Reclaim

Human-approved outreach · Claude, Slack, event-sourced tracking

The problem

Software spend quietly leaks through seats nobody uses anymore, but a proper, ongoing review of every license was too heavy a process to run often — so it mostly didn't happen. The only time anyone looked closely was during a slow, manual, once-in-a-while audit.

How it works

1Export seats from the vendor console
2AI proposes likely-inactive candidates
3A person reviews & edits the list
4Approved batch goes out, attributed by name
5Holder replies Keep / Release / something else
6IT reclaims the seat manually

The tool turns a vendor's raw seat export into a reviewable shortlist: an AI step flags people who look inactive or who self-reported they don't need the tool anymore, but nothing is sent until a person looks at that list and can remove anyone who shouldn't be on it. Once approved, the batch goes out over chat through a personal, attributable channel, so every outreach is traceable to the specific person who sent it — never a shared, anonymous bot identity.

Recipients get a short set of response options, and a live dashboard tracks every batch from "in progress" to "complete," with an audit view for anything that didn't get a clean answer. An earlier, spreadsheet-based version of this tool got rebuilt entirely after it broke under real concurrent use — a shared spreadsheet's write-lock couldn't safely handle simultaneous responses, and non-standard characters broke its signature checking. The rebuilt version tracks every decision as its own event rather than a cell to overwrite, so answers arriving at the same moment can't corrupt each other.

Decisions that mattered

The bot was deliberately built to never take the final action itself — it doesn't touch a vendor console, remove a license, or change a group membership on its own. It proposes and asks; a person always pulls the trigger on removing a seat. Silence is never treated as a "yes" to releasing a license, and an out-of-office signal pauses the clock instead of escalating.

Outcome

What used to be an occasional, time-consuming audit became a routine that takes minutes to kick off and runs itself from there, with a visible trail of who was asked, what they said, and who followed up. It has since been run successfully by a second person, independently.

What it intentionally doesn't do

No automatic removals, no silent-consent assumptions, no manager escalation on non-response — every edge case routes back to a person, by design.

OPS-04 · Automation & AI

Automated Vulnerability Remediation Digest

Security operations · Apps Script, Zapier, generative AI, Slack

The problem

A weekly endpoint-security vulnerability report arrived as a dense spreadsheet attachment, and turning it into "here's exactly who I need to message and what they should do" took real time every single week — time that scaled with the size of the fleet, not with the actual risk.

How it works

1Report lands in a shared inbox
2Automation extracts & loads it into a tracked sheet
3AI drafts a plain-English fix per issue
4Formatted digest posts to the team channel
5One click DMs the affected person
6Weekly trend logs itself automatically

An automation watches a dedicated inbox for the weekly report, unpacks the attachment, and loads the raw data into a spreadsheet that already knows how to map each affected device to the person using it. A generative-AI formula layer turns each vulnerability into a short, plain-English fix a non-technical person can follow, with a special case for one hardware vendor's own update tool.

A scheduled script then posts a clean, per-device summary into the team's channel, with a "notify this person" button on each one, and appends that week's total to a running trend log with a chart leadership can check at any time.

Decisions that mattered

Notifying the affected person stayed a one-click, human-triggered action instead of a fully automatic send. Vulnerability data lags real patch state — a device can already be fixed and simply not have reported back yet — so a two-second glance before the message goes out avoids telling someone to fix something that's already fixed, which matters more for trust than the few seconds it costs.

Outcome

A multi-hour weekly spreadsheet exercise became a few minutes of review plus one click per device that actually needs attention, with a standing trend chart nobody has to build by hand.

What it intentionally doesn't do

It doesn't send anything without a human click, doesn't yet track whether someone actually remediated afterward (the next planned step), and never writes back to the security tool — it only reads and re-presents.

OPS-05 · Automation & AI

Agentic Documentation & Support Assistant

Support & knowledge · LLM agent, Slack, ticketing handoff

The problem

Good documentation existed, but it lived in several different places, and plenty of chat questions were things already written down somewhere — if only someone had time to go find it. Meanwhile documentation quietly drifted out of date, because updating it wasn't anyone's job in particular.

How it works

1Person asks a question in chat
2AI checks real knowledge sources
3Confident answer → posted in-thread, instantly
4Not confident → opens a ticket with full context
5Resolution confirmed, or escalated to a person

A person's question in chat is handed to an AI agent that checks it against the team's actual documentation and knowledge sources before answering anything. If it finds a grounded, confident answer, it replies right there in the thread — available any time, not just during working hours.

If it can't, it doesn't guess: it opens a properly-described ticket and hands the conversation to a person with full context attached, so nobody has to re-explain their problem from scratch after getting a non-answer from a bot. A quick satisfaction check at the end of each exchange closes the loop, or escalates to a guided, human-supported resolution path if the answer didn't actually land.

Decisions that mattered

The one rule that shaped everything else: the agent is allowed to say "I don't know" and hand off, but it is not allowed to guess with confidence. That single constraint is what makes the answers it does give trustworthy enough that people keep using it instead of going straight to a person.

Outcome

Routine, already-documented questions get resolved instantly and outside office hours, and the questions that genuinely need a person arrive with full context already attached instead of a blank "can someone help?" message.

What it intentionally doesn't do

It's a first line of response, not a replacement for a person — anything outside documented, confident territory still goes to a human, every time, by design.

OPS-06 · Physical Operations

Global Hardware & Asset Lifecycle Operations

Multi-region logistics · procurement, MDM, warehousing, returns

The problem

Getting a laptop into a new hire's hands — and getting it back cleanly when they left — across five-plus countries meant juggling several regional vendors, warehouses, and procurement paths, with no single place that showed the real state of an order. Inventory ran low without warning, and the process leaned on a handful of people remembering how things worked, region by region.

How it works

1New-hire data collected from HR sources
2Logged into one shared tracking board
3Hardware ordered via regional procurement
4Shipped to the nearest regional warehouse
5Delivered & enrolled into device management
6Offboarding reverses it: wipe → return → refurbish

New-hire details get pulled from whichever of several HR sources has them first — a weekly report, a request ticket, a spreadsheet — into one shared board that becomes the single place anyone can check order status, shipping, and what's still outstanding. Hardware requests flow through a standard procurement-and-approval path into region-specific fulfillment warehouses, each with its own carrier and paperwork quirks — customs declarations for some regions, different return-label and pickup processes for others — documented as a runbook per region rather than tribal knowledge in one person's head.

On delivery, devices enroll into mobile device management with a consistent naming convention, keeping asset tracking, support, and audits all pointing at the same record for the same machine. Offboarding runs the pipeline in reverse: a remote wipe command goes out the moment a termination is confirmed, a return kit and label ship automatically, and the warehouse refurbishes and restocks what comes back.

Decisions that mattered

Explicit low-inventory thresholds per region replaced the old approach of noticing a stockout only after someone's start date was at risk. Exceptions to the standard hardware list — a region where the standard spec isn't available, for instance — stayed possible, but only through a documented approval step with a named sign-off, so "special cases" didn't quietly become the new normal.

Outcome

Day-one hardware readiness became predictable across every region instead of a recurring fire drill, emergency procurement requests dropped, and the whole process became something a new team member could pick up from documentation instead of shadowing someone for months.

What it intentionally doesn't do

It doesn't remove the need for regional vendor relationships or judgment on edge cases — it removes the need to reinvent the process, or rediscover who to call, every single time.
